# What Mavis Beacon Teaches Typing teaches, and how

Research for [What Mavis Beacon Teaches Typing teaches, and how](https://github.com/k-electron/astro-type/issues/180), under the map [Phase 3: a curriculum that teaches, with practice that fits the player](https://github.com/k-electron/astro-type/issues/175). Written 2026-11-26.

**Question:** what does Mavis Beacon Teaches Typing teach, and how? Its lessons (their order and size), its games and the skill each one trains, its adaptive and custom lessons and how they pick weak keys, its technique coaching, its goals and how it measures progress, and what reviewers and studies say worked and what didn't.

There are two lesson engines under the one name. The first runs from 1987 through version 20 and the 2020 edition (and Software MacKiev's Mac and academic editions). The second, "Powered by UltraKey", is a different course sold beside it. _Inference:_ the 2026 browser edition mixes the two: its 16 game names are version 20's 16 games, while its "100+ skill checks" and certificate "after 8 hours of practice" are UltraKey's claims. Its guide is locked to licence holders, so I couldn't read how its lessons work.

**How much to trust each source:**

- **Primary:** the publisher's manuals, user guides, teacher guides and help file, and its current product pages and screenshots. They are the publisher's own word on how the program behaves, so they aren't independent. Where they claim results ("Proven positive results in just eight hours"), I quote the claim and don't treat it as evidence.
- **Read with care:** the section the 1987 manual calls "The Theory Behind Mavis" appears almost word for word in the 1995 manual and the 2008 Teacher Guide. That shows the publisher described the same design for 20 years. It doesn't prove the 2008 program still ran the 1987 logic. The guides of one edition sometimes disagree with each other; I say so where it matters.
- **Weaker, marked _(review)_:** reviews in established magazines: START (November 1989, Atari ST edition), COMPUTE! (December 1992, Version 2.0 for Multimedia), Macworld (July 2009, the 2009 Deluxe Edition for Mac) and PCWorld (October 2010, Platinum 25th Anniversary Edition). Each is one reviewer on one edition.
- **Studies:** I found no peer-reviewed study of how well Mavis Beacon teaches. Section 8 lists what I searched and the two papers I found but couldn't read. None is used for a claim.
- **Not used:** Wikipedia, blogs, forums, shop listings, typing-site reviews, and the New York Times' 1987 review, which I couldn't open.

**Terms:** this note uses the names in `GLOSSARY.md`: Stage, Sector, Run, Keystroke, First attempt, Miss, Mix-up, Letter pair, Rhythm, Shift hand, Star bar, Logbook. Mavis Beacon's own words ("lesson", "section quiz", "hot keys", "Chalkboard") are quoted as its words. Each section keeps **what the sources say** apart from **inference** (my reading, marked _Inference_).

## Bottom line

1. **The path is fixed, small-stepped, and puts capitals before numbers.** Platinum 20 and the 2011 Mac edition show the same course: a Beginner level of ten lessons that brings in the letters two to four keys at a time (`asdf`, `jkl;`, `ei`, `gh`, `ru`, `ty`, `qwop`, `vbmn`, `zxc`, `.,`), then an Intermediate level of fourteen that starts with Left Shift and Right Shift, then `?":!`, then the digits in groups of two or three, then the symbols in groups of two to four, and an Advanced level that is one diagnostic. Capitals before numbers is the order the Phase 3 vision already asks for. After the home row, most letter lessons add the same reach on both hands at once (`ei`, `gh`, `ru`, `ty`, `qwop`, `vbmn`).
2. **Each step is gated by a quiz, and accuracy comes first.** "You may skip practice lessons but not quizzes. In order to progress through the typing lessons, each section quiz must be successfully completed" (2005). In the Beginner lessons "speed is ignored entirely" (1995), and the speed goal "will only apply once you have achieved the advanced level and that all the keys have been introduced" (2005). A reviewer found that "many of the end-of-lesson quizzes" demanded 100% accuracy and called it "Obsessive insistence on perfection" _(review, Macworld 2009)_. No edition I read publishes the quiz's pass mark.
3. **The adaptive engine, as documented in 1987, is close to the Phase 3 orchestrator.** It picks "hot keys" from each key's speed and accuracy, weighted three ways: extra weight for keys in the current lesson, weight in proportion to how common the key is in English, and extra weight for keys it has rarely practised. It then looks for a standout kind of mistake among about thirty (wrong row, wrong column, mirror image, Shift, dropped, doubled, transposed and more). If one stands out, the lesson is built to fix it. If not, it looks for a weak finger, row, hand or kind of character, and failing that it builds a general lesson. It avoids repeating a lesson type, explains every choice on its "Chalkboard", lets the player take another suggestion, and watches for frustration (quitting a lesson part way, fidgeting, "lots of keystrokes coming through very fast, none of which are correct").
4. **Its "weak keys" are inferred from which key was pressed, never from seeing fingers.** Mirror, wrong-row and wrong-column errors, and the per-finger and per-hand error charts, all come from the key expected and the key pressed. That is the data astro-type's Flight log already holds.
5. **Games changed role.** In 1987 the race game was one format a lesson could take: Mavis chose it, and a drill could be swapped for the game with the same lesson plan. From 2005 on, the games are a separate area, and "The activities in Typing Games are not connected to the feature in the program that monitors typing progress" (2005, 2008, 2020). Each game names one skill: accuracy, speed, rhythm, letter patterns or the number pad. Only some of them adapt to the player's speed goal.
6. **Technique is taught in words, pictures and error remedies, and none of it is checked.** The manuals cover posture, the home row, light strokes ("pretend that the keys on the keyboard are 'red-hot'"), returning home, eyes on the screen and opposite-hand Shift. On screen, "guide hands" show the finger for each key, and a Keys and Fingers tool shows the finger for any key pressed. Error categories carry technique advice (wrong row: "reposition your hands over the 'Home Row'"). A reviewer noted the guide hands go unwatched because "you're too busy looking at what you are typing" _(review, START 1989)_.
7. **Goals and measures:** a speed goal in words per minute (by 2020 the default is 40, adjustable from 25 to 120). The 1987 manual sets a first goal of 12 WPM, then 15. Reports show WPM, an adjusted WPM that "penalizes you for errors by subtracting one keystroke for each mistake" (1992), accuracy as the percent of characters typed correctly in quizzes and tests (2008), a keyboard coloured red, blue and green by how well each key is known, and a curriculum map with a certificate per level. The 1987, 1995 and 2008 manuals all recommend "about one hour a day; not more, not less", citing unnamed studies.
8. **Evidence that it works is thin.** Reviewers praised how quickly the lessons "start closing in on problem areas" while mixing in lessons "I could do really well on" _(review, START 1989)_. They criticised the 100% quizzes, guide hands nobody watches, and counting every key after a mistyped letter as another error _(review, COMPUTE! 1992)_. I found no controlled study of Mavis Beacon's teaching.

## 1. The lessons: their order and size

### What the sources say

**The key order (Platinum 20 and 2011 Mac).** Both editions show a "Curriculum Map" of three levels. I read both screenshots. Platinum 20 labels each box ("Introducing 'asdf'", "Adding 'gh'", "'qwop' Keys", "Learning 'zxc'", "The Number Keys '456'"):

| Level        | Lessons, in order                                                                                                                                           | Count |
| :----------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :---- |
| Beginner     | `asdf` → `jkl;` → `ei` → `gh` → `ru` → `ty` → `qwop` → `vbmn` → `zxc` → `.,`                                                                                  | 10    |
| Intermediate | Left Shift → Right Shift → `?":!` → `123` → `456` → `78` → `90` → `@#$` → `%^` → `&*` → `()-_` → `+=` → `{}[]` → `<>/`                                          | 14    |
| Advanced     | Advanced Diagnostic                                                                                                                                         | 1     |

The 2011 Mac edition (a UK keyboard) differs only in its keyboard's keys: its fourth lesson is `gh'` and it has `@£$` where Platinum 20 has `@#$`. A star after each level prints a certificate, and the last star prints the "completion certificate".

**The levels in words.** The 1995 manual says: "The Beginner level introduces you to all of the alphabet keys, emphasizing accuracy in those keys; throughout the Beginner lessons, speed is ignored entirely." Then: "Knowledge of and proficiency with all of the alphabet keys puts you in the Intermediate category. Here, Mavis will review keyboard fundamentals and give you some practice text to type. Next, she will begin introducing the number and symbol keys. Once you've learned them, it's time to work on improving your typing speed and refining your new skills. At this point, Mavis will begin to analyze your typing and suggest lessons to help you improve, all the way through the Advanced category." The 2008 Teacher Guide repeats this, except that it drops "speed is ignored entirely" and says Beginners "must increase their speed through accuracy training before they can proceed to the next level."

**Where a player starts.** 1987: the player picks Beginner, Intermediate or Advanced; someone who types "not faster than about 20 words per minute" should still pick Beginner. From 2005 on, the first visit to the lessons offers a diagnostic ("Test Typing Level" in 2005, "Test My Typing Level" in 2008 and 2020) or the first Beginner lesson; 2008 and 2020 add that a player who skips the test "will not have another opportunity to take the diagnostic quiz". The 2008 Teacher Guide places anyone slower than 30 WPM in Beginner.

**Lesson size.** The curriculum map shows the size of each step in new keys (two to four). Inside a step, the documents say less:

- "Each session contains several different lessons that are relatively short in length. Since your skill level and typing goals affect your performance, the length of time varies." (2005)
- "You may skip practice lessons but not quizzes. In order to progress through the typing lessons, each section quiz must be successfully completed." (2005); "You must successfully complete section quizzes in order to progress through the typing lessons." (2008, 2020)
- "You are automatically moved up to the next skill level once you have completed your current skill level." (2020)
- "Your typing score and analysis information are only saved when the entire lesson is completed." (2008, 2020)
- UltraKey describes its own course as: "Learn New Keys One Stage at a time. Systematically develop new keystrokes, blending them into pairs, chains, words and sentences", and "For each lesson you complete, a corresponding skill check uses real-word typing challenges to ensure your progress."

**What the 1987 edition said instead.** The 1987 manual describes no fixed sequence. Each lesson "addresses a particular typing problem (such as poor speed or dropped characters)", chosen on the Chalkboard. Until Mavis has data, "both her recommendations and her lesson generation are driven from prestored data. Like a computer chess program in the early going, the program plays from 'book.'"

### Inference

- **Capitals before numbers is Mavis Beacon's order too.** Shift comes straight after the last letter lesson, and both Shift keys come before any digit. Phase 3's order (home row, top, bottom, capitals with opposite-hand Shift, then numbers and symbols) matches it. Mavis puts sentence punctuation (`?":!`) between Shift and the digits.
- **Mirrored finger pairs.** After the home row (one hand per lesson), most letter steps add the same reach on both hands: `ei` (middle fingers up), `gh` and `ty` (index fingers stretching in), `ru` (index fingers up), `qwop` (little and ring fingers up) and `vbmn` (index fingers down). Only `zxc` (left hand) and `.,` (right hand) are one-sided. So 26 letters arrive in ten lessons, and each reach is practised on both hands together. The cost is the "mirror image" error that Mavis itself names (section 3): a learner given `e` and `i` together has two keys in the same row, typed by the same finger of opposite hands, to confuse. astro-type could test this in the simulated beginner before choosing between mirrored pairs and one hand at a time.
- **Step size.** Two to four new keys per step, ten steps for the letters, fourteen for Shift, digits and symbols. That gives a scale for "one small new thing per step" (P4): about 25 steps for the full keyboard if a step means "a new key group".
- **The adaptive part starts late.** Both 1995 and 2008 say Mavis analyses and suggests lessons after the numbers and symbols are learned. The fixed path covers the whole keyboard first. The 2008 Teacher Guide also says tailoring starts "once students have completed a few pre-scripted lessons and the program has an adequate sampling of their typing", so the two statements don't fully agree. Neither says whether weak-key lessons interrupt the Beginner path.

## 2. What a lesson is, and the gate between steps

### What the sources say

- **Practice lessons and quizzes.** Practice lessons can be skipped; "section quizzes" can't (2005, 2008, 2020).
- **The quiz's pass mark is not published** in any edition I read. A reviewer of the 2009 Mac edition wrote: "There is also an insistence on 100 percent accuracy with many of the end-of-lesson quizzes. Should you miss just one character in the test passage, you're booted right back for more practice." The review lists "Obsessive insistence on perfection" among its cons _(review, Macworld 2009)_.
- **Accuracy over speed.** The 2008 Teacher Guide: "The program scores student work based on typing accuracy and speed. However, it gives accuracy far more weight than speed, especially with beginning typists." It also advises judging each learner by "progress achieved" rather than "fixed speed or accuracy criteria".
- **The speed goal waits for the whole keyboard.** "Enter a number for your Typing Speed Goal. The goal will only apply once you have achieved the advanced level and that all the keys have been introduced." (2005)
- **Wrong keys.** 1992: "Usually, errors are accepted. If you make a mistake, the typing cursor moves forward and the error stays on the screen… For some lessons, Mavis wants to be sure that you type each letter correctly. Then she sets Drill mode, which does not move the cursor forward if you make an error. You must press the correct key to proceed." The 1987 "Pattern Drill" goes further: "You must type the entire pattern correctly before moving on, or the cursor will move back to the beginning of the pattern."
- **Backspace.** 1995: "At the Beginning level, Mavis turns the Backspace key off, because a perfect typist never makes mistakes!" The 2008 User Guide's class defaults set "Backspace Key: Off" (and today's Classroom Edition help still does), but the 2008 Teacher Guide says "by default, the Backspace key is on in the program". The two guides of one edition disagree.
- **Retrying.** A player can retake any of their last ten lessons from Lesson History (2005, 2008).

### Inference

- **A Mavis step has the same shape as a Phase 3 step: practice, then a gate.** Practice inside a step is optional and the gate is not. The Phase 3 vision's "a player stuck below a Star bar always has a way through that is the practice they need" goes further than Mavis does: Mavis documents no help for a player who keeps failing a quiz, beyond retaking practice lessons.
- **A near-100% gate is the one thing a reviewer singled out as a flaw.** astro-type's Star bars are 90, 95 and 98% on First attempts; Mavis's quizzes (as reviewed) sit above even the 3-Star bar. That supports keeping the gate below perfection, which is how the Star bars already work. Changing the bars is the owner's call, not this note's.
- **"Speed ignored until all keys are introduced" is a stronger form of P2.** Today every Stage has WPM bars for 2 and 3 Stars. Mavis Beacon (1995, 2005) asks for no speed at all while keys are being learned. If the owner ever wants to follow it, it would be a rule change to raise, not a design choice.
- **astro-type already works in Mavis's "Drill mode".** A Miss leaves the Drone in place until the right key is pressed, as Drill mode keeps the cursor. That is the stricter of Mavis's two modes, and the one it uses when it "wants to be sure that you type each letter correctly".

## 3. Adaptive lessons: how Mavis picks weak keys and mistakes

### What the sources say

All quotes are from "The Theory Behind Mavis" (1987), unless marked. The 1995 manual repeats it almost word for word. The 2008 Teacher Guide repeats most of it, with the differences noted.

**Choosing keys.** "The first thing Mavis does is analyze the results of previous lessons for speed and accuracy per key. Then Mavis determines which keys to drill using a special formula to determine key 'hot spots.' This calculation examines all keys, giving extra weight to those keys that occurred in the current lesson, proportional weight to characters as they occur in common English usage, and an inverse weight based upon the frequency of use in the lessons Mavis has so far prepared for the user." The three reasons:

1. "Mavis wants to emphasize keys the student has worked with a lot this lesson… By emphasizing the current session, these keys are emphasized for the remainder of the session and the typist recognizes that the goal is being worked toward."
2. "Mavis cares about the keys in proportion to their frequency in common English usage. If a typist types the letter Z half as fast as the letter S, but S occurs three times more frequently than Z, the letter S needs the most work, not Z."
3. "Mavis wants to make sure that all keys are covered. So, she keeps track of all the keys she has included in her lessons, builds a frequency table, and biases the choice in favor of keys that haven't been practiced much."

"These three criteria are in fact contradictory… but the rules used mediate between them and everything ultimately balances out." The weights themselves are never given. The 1992 help file describes the same thing as a "Problem Keys" graph: "Along with speed and accuracy, Mavis considers how often the character occurs in written English… Mavis also considers the number of times each character was tested in past sessions so you get a chance to practice the entire keyboard… Keys that require the most work have the biggest bars." The 2008 Teacher Guide still mentions the weight for keys used in earlier lessons, but its list of reasons keeps only the first two.

**Before there is data.** Until Mavis has "enough typing and other response data", lessons come from "prestored data" (1987). The 2008 guides add that a red key on the keyboard report can be one "that your student has not used enough. The program needs to collect a certain amount of data before it can report on performance." The amount isn't given.

**Classifying the mistake.** "She first analyzes the results of the lesson for many kinds of errors and statistics like speed and accuracy per key, number of keys needed before catching an error and backing up, the transition rate to and from a given key, dropped keys, doubled keys, transpositions, a hit that is the right row, but the wrong finger, or is the right column but the wrong key, overlapping errors (typing between two keys instead of on one), mirror image (correct row and column but wrong hand), 'Shift Key' problems or any other kind of consistent mis-hitting problem. There are about thirty categories to begin with… Each of these statistics is maintained for each key. Next, the program groups the results into several 'compositional' categories according to the statistic's relation to any finger, row, column, hand or key type."

The manuals' "Typing Error Glossary" names nineteen of these categories (1987, 1995): poor accuracy, backspacing, doubled, dropped, finger, hand, migration, mirror, mis-hit, number key, overlap, recovery, Shift key, poor speed, substitution, symbol key, transposition, wrong column and wrong row. Some examples in Mavis's words:

- "MIRROR: A 'mirror-image' typing error happens when you type a key in the correct row and column but with the wrong hand… what you wanted to type was an I, with the same finger of your right hand."
- "WRONG ROW: … A frequent mistake of this type is typing F for R, or vice versa. The cure for this is usually to reposition your hands over the 'Home Row.'"
- "WRONG COLUMN: Occurs when you type a letter in the correct row, but in the wrong vertical column. For instance, if you typed an R when you meant to type a T."
- "FINGER: Major typing errors can be traced to the keys struck by a single finger… Mavis will help you address this problem with lessons that center on your weak fingers."

The 1992 help file shows the same analysis as graphs: errors by hand, by finger, by row, and per-key graphs for row, column, mirror, Shift, backspace, substitution, doubling, dropped, transposition, between-key and "recovery after errors" ("how quickly the typist notices an error after making it"). It adds: "Often, typists have the most difficulty with the pinks and index fingers."

**Choosing the lesson.** "Mavis scans to locate letters with the highest 'hot key' rating… Next Mavis totals across the rows for keystroking errors in those columns that match her selected 'hot keys.' These totals are adjusted by the frequency the category has been used as a contributing factor in determining previous lessons and by a preference weighting based upon our research into the causes, and importance, of each kind of error… If there is a standout, it is set aside." Otherwise: "Mavis looks for 'descriptive' errors. She looks to see if errors can be categorized by finger, row, hand or character type (punctuation, numerics, alphas, etc.)… If Mavis still cannot locate any particular category, she creates a 'no category' lesson."

**Building it.** "Each error type has one or more associated general lesson 'templates' which are lists of rules specifying an appropriate response in general terms… Mavis chooses a 'least recently used' remedy selection". The text comes from "her extensive database of words… words containing certain characters or certain patterns or both", from sayings, facts, riddles and jokes, from "the five hundred most common words in English", or from left-hand, right-hand and "typing hurdles" lessons (words that "challenge your fingers to move up and back among the keyboard's rows", 1987 card).

**Not repeating, and frustration.** "Mavis Beacon tries hard to avoid repetition. She is always against doing a primary lesson type at too closely spaced an interval… [she will] instead predict that unless the typist shapes up, they will probably see this lesson again soon. Mavis is also concerned about the frustration factor. Sometimes the user gives up (escapes out of the lesson only partway through), fidgets (lots of pauses to change settings, or often does not accept the offered lesson), or just gets upset (Mavis sees lots of keystrokes coming through very fast, none of which are correct). At such points, Mavis may suggest playing a game, revising the target speed goal or even calling it a day." The 2008 Teacher Guide keeps the three signs but drops the sentence saying what Mavis does about them. The 1992 help file has settings for both: "Frustration Noticed" and "Past Lessons Considered" ("If on, she will consider not only the immediate previous lesson, but all earlier lessons").

**Explaining, and letting the player choose.** "You will see the problem Mavis has chosen to work on at the top of the chalkboard. Underneath that, Mavis writes her 'remedy'… A longer explanation of her recommendation appears in the middle of the chalkboard" (1987). The player can accept, or choose "Change Drill To Game", "Make Another Suggestion" (the "next best" lesson), "Original Suggestion", "Repeat Last Suggestion" or "Create My Own Lesson" (1987 card).

**Later editions.** The 2015 and 2020 guides describe the keyboard report (2015: "Mavis will tailor lessons to give your student more practice on the red keys") but don't explain how lessons are chosen. The 2026 browser edition advertises "Fix My Weak Keys: targeted practice on the keys you miss most", with no published method.

### Inference

- **Mavis Beacon is prior art for most of the Phase 3 orchestrator.** The vision's choices line up with Mavis's documented ones: the Stage's own keys first (Mavis's "current lesson" weight), weak keys next, weighted by English frequency (Mavis's second criterion, with the same S-against-Z reasoning), waiting for enough data before acting ("plays from 'book'"), shaping the loop around a kind of mistake that stands out (wrong hand, wrong row, a Mix-up), a reason shown with every suggestion (the Chalkboard), the player free to refuse (Alternatives), and the same three signs of frustration (quitting, pausing, bursts of wrong keys). That shows the vision's shape has a long-selling precedent. It doesn't show the shape works: Mavis never published its weights, and no study tested them.
- **Mavis adds one thing the vision doesn't name: coverage.** Its third criterion favours keys it has rarely practised, so that every key keeps coming up. The decision ticket on the orchestrator's evidence could choose whether to have that term.
- **Two-phase choice: kind of mistake first, then where.** Mavis first asks "is there a standout kind of error?" and only then "is there a weak finger, row, hand or kind of key?", and builds a general lesson if neither. That order could be how a loop's purpose is chosen.
- **Every one of Mavis's finger, row and hand categories can be computed from astro-type's data.** Mirror, wrong row and wrong column need only the key expected and the key pressed, which every Keystroke in the Flight log has and the Logbook keeps as Mix-ups. The per-finger and per-hand charts add only the curriculum's key and finger table. Like astro-type (P14), Mavis infers finger problems from keys and never claims to see fingers. Mavis's doubled, dropped and transposed letters assume the cursor moves on after a wrong key, which astro-type's Drones don't. Their nearest equivalents would be a Miss whose key is the character just typed (doubled) or the next one (dropped or transposed). Those need the neighbouring characters, which the Flight log has but a Mix-up pair doesn't.
- **"Avoid repeating a lesson type" and "least recently used remedy"** are a simple, deterministic way to vary loops without anything random at play time.
- **"Past Lessons Considered"** shows Mavis offered both "last lesson only" and "all lessons" as a setting. The vision has already settled this (overall progress, not single Runs).

## 4. Custom lessons and lesson formats

### What the sources say

**1987 ("Create My Own Lesson", on the card):**

- Emphasis: Speed or Accuracy ("the lesson will be geared to concentrate on either speed or accuracy, ignoring the other"), Left Hand or Right Hand, Hurdles, or General.
- Specific Characters: "type in 1-10 characters from the keyboard. The lesson will be made up of words containing these characters."
- Length, a time limit, a target speed ("This sets the speed of the metronome (for a lesson) or the race car (for a game)"), and the text source (including "My Own File").
- Lesson types: Regular Drill; Barrier Drill ("you can only look ahead eight characters"); Transcription (typed from a printout); Pattern Drill ("Common letter patterns will be highlighted… You must type the entire pattern correctly before moving on, or the cursor will move back to the beginning of the pattern"); Shift Key Drill ("Every word in the lesson will start with a capital letter"); Guided Key Tour ("for beginners. Here, Mavis explains which finger should type which key as you type along. You must type each letter correctly before the tour continues"); and Race Game.
- The Theory section explains Barrier typing: "only the next few characters (about one second's worth at the current average typing speed) of the lesson are shown to the typist. This keeps the typist from reading too far ahead and emphasizes the notion of grouping characters into commonly occurring chunks." And: "She can offer 'speed' and 'accuracy' lessons in which accuracy or speed (respectively) of the typing is not recorded as part of the user's permanent record."

**1992** adds Dictation (typing from recorded speech) and lesson length "in words (of five letters average length)".

**2005 to 2020 ("Custom Lesson Designer"):** a lesson has a type (Standard, Speed Test, Transcription, or one of the games with the lesson's text), content (Mavis's texts, practice texts or an imported file), limits (time, "the maximum number of errors that are allowed before the lesson ends", number of words) and goals (WPM and "the minimum percentage of accuracy that students must meet to successfully complete the lesson") (2008). Speed tests draw from "a set list of 20,000 words" (2015). The Practice Area offers texts to type at will; by 2015 it includes "the full text of dozens of classic novels" and news feeds.

### Inference

- **Formats that train one skill each** (Barrier for reading ahead in chunks, Pattern for common letter sequences, Shift Key Drill for capitals, Guided Key Tour for finger-to-key) are ready-made ideas for the generator's loop Stages. Pattern Drill is the nearest to a Letter-pair loop.
- **"Not recorded as part of the user's permanent record"**: Mavis let a speed-only lesson leave accuracy out of the record, and the reverse. Whether a loop Stage's Run counts fully in the Logbook is a question for the progress ticket; astro-type today folds every cleared Run in.

## 5. Games as practice, and the skill each one trains

### What the sources say

**1987:** one arcade game, the "Road-Racer", against "Red Walter": "Typing well, you leap forward and your opponent fades into your rear view mirror. You make mistakes, and 'Red Walter' starts to creep up on you." The box also lists musical "Tempo-Typing" with an on-screen metronome ("Studies have shown that use of a steady beat helps new typists increase their typing speed… Rhythm helps us break through these 'typing walls'"). The game was a lesson format: Mavis "might decide it's time for a special challenge", the player could "Change Drill To Game" with the same lesson, and Mavis "keeps account of the ratio of drills to games and whether the user continually requests the game. If so, she adjusts the ratio to match". The 1992 help: the pace car travels "usually just a little bit faster that you have been typing to give you a challenge"; "Occasionally, Mavis suggests a game as a change of pace from the lesson flow", and a setting chooses how often. By 2005 this setting is "Lessons Taken as Games: Never, Sometimes, or Often".

**2005 on:** the games are their own area. "The activities in Typing Games are not connected to the feature in the program that monitors your typing progress" (2005, 2008, 2020). The 2015 guide counts how this grew: "the first edition, which featured just two simple typing games… This new Software MacKiev edition comes with 17 challenging arcade-style games". The 2008 User Guide describes 16, each with the skill it names and whether it gets harder as the player's speed goal rises:

_(review)_ PCWorld 2010: the games "are all some variation on 'type properly or bad things happen,' but there's enough variety and focus on different skills that they remain a fun practice mechanism… they require you to watch the screen, not the keyboard, which is exactly what a good typing game should do." Macworld 2009 found the games "aren't the most sophisticated but do add a layer of fun".

### Inference

- **Mavis moved games out of the measured course.** From 2005 its games are an unmeasured side area, and its guides warn about learners escaping into them. astro-type has no such split: every Stage is played as the game, and every cleared Run is measured (P1), so there is no unmeasured place to escape to. The sources don't say why Mavis made the split.
- **Most Mavis games name one skill.** A loop Stage aimed at one skill (accuracy, rhythm, letter patterns) has a precedent in them. Far Off Adventures rewards a steady rhythm over speed, the same idea as Overdrive filling from Rhythm, which is out of scope for this phase.
- **Bubble Pop judges timing** (a key counts only inside the "Typing Zone"). astro-type rejected timing Judgements (P1: "a hit is a hit"), so this is one to leave.
- **Only some games scale to the speed goal.** That is a simple model of Drone pressure as target speed (P1), which is also out of this phase.

## 6. Technique coaching: posture, hands and fingers

### What the sources say

**Posture and stroke (1987, repeated in 1995 and 2008):**

- "Sit with your feet firmly on the floor, twelve inches or so apart. Your upper arms almost vertical; elbows hanging freely… you will sit with the middle of your body opposite the M."
- "Let your fingers lightly touch the second row of keys above the 'Space Bar.' This row of keys is called the 'Home Row,' and you'll begin and end all your typing strokes from this position. Your fingers should curl naturally over the keyboard, and your right thumb should touch the 'Space Bar' lightly."
- "Stroke the keys and the 'Space Bar' with the weight of your fingers only, not with the force of your wrists and arms… pretend that the keys on the keyboard are 'red-hot,' that your fingers will be burned if you don't touch the keys lightly and briefly. Do not, however, let your fingers bounce on the keys. Keep your wrists flat."
- From 1995: breaks, stretching, monitor height and "Keep your hands and wrists straight".

**Fingers and Shift (1987):** "the left index finger strikes the F, G, R, T, V, B, 4, 5 keys." "The correct technique is to hold down the 'Shift Key' nearest the pinky finger of your other hand. For example, if you want to type an uppercase F, you should strike the F key with your left index finger, while holding down the right 'Shift Key' with the pinky of your right hand."

**Speed through control (1987, 1995, 2008):** "The secret of typing speedily lies in the flexibility and control of each finger. This includes how fast each of your fingers types a key and how fast it returns to its 'Home Row' position… real speed can only come after you have learned accuracy. The principle is this: Increase your speed to a higher level only after you have gained complete control at a lower level… If you make many errors, you're typing faster than your ability to control your keystrokes."

**Eyes on the screen.** "Don't look down at your hands as you go; look up at the screen" (1987). The 2008 Teacher Guide's first activity has learners cover the keys with a box or tape, with "a 'peek-free' touch typing goal", and its assessment advice for beginners: "Don't emphasize speed and accuracy yet. Do assess posture at the keyboard and, after a couple of lessons, assess improvement in the students' efforts to avoid looking at the keyboard while typing. Don't confuse a beginning typist's frustration with poor attitude."

**On screen:**

- Guide hands: in 1987 "As you begin to type, so will the 'guide' hands", and "the keys you press will also guide you"; they can be turned off "should you just want to use the lettered key tops", or both off as "the ultimate test". In 2008 they "show your student where their fingers should be placed".
- Keys and Fingers (1987), later the Finger Positioning Guide (2008, 2020): press any key and the hands and a sentence show which finger types it. The 1987 Guided Key Tour walks a beginner through the keyboard, key by key.
- Videos: by 2015 "a video instruction center, and ergonomics lessons"; UltraKey: "A virtual model demonstrates correct body and hand position" and "Video and interactive graphics teach you typing technique, finger names and home row position"; 2026: "Posture and ergonomics video instruction".

**Error remedies with technique advice (1987 glossary):** wrong row ("reposition your hands over the 'Home Row'"), overlap ("often occurs when you are rushed and your sitting and hand positions are not correct. Concentrate on short, rhythmic keystrokes"), Shift ("depress the 'Shift Keys' in rhythm with the rest of your key typing"), backspacing ("Because backspacing requires a long reach from the 'Home Row,' Mavis Beacon watches for errors caused by that hand motion").

_(review)_ START 1989: "Generally, however, the shadow hands aren't very useful, since you're too busy looking at what you are typing to see what the hands do anyway."

### Inference

- **Mavis teaches technique and never claims to check it,** as P14 asks. Its one indirect check is the error categories: a wrong-row or mirror pattern is read as a hand-position problem and answered with advice and a targeted lesson. That is one way the owner's "right fingers" aim can be served: a loop chosen from Mix-ups that point at a finger or hand problem, with the technique tip that fits it.
- **The tip is tied to the error, not only to the Stage.** Mavis attaches advice to a kind of mistake ("reposition your hands over the Home Row" for wrong-row errors). astro-type's Stages carry one technique tip each today. A loop's tip could come from the mistake that triggered the loop.
- **The guide hands go unwatched once the learner reads the text** (one reviewer's view). That is consistent with P3's plan to fade the hands as keys are learned (a later phase).
- **Opposite-hand Shift is taught in words only.** astro-type already measures Shift hand; no Mavis source describes measuring it (its "Shift Key" errors mean the wrong case or a symbol for a digit). That is one place astro-type can coach what Mavis could only describe.

## 7. Goals, and how progress is measured

### What the sources say

**Speed goal.**

- 1987: "The program will choose a target (or goal) speed for you, based on your typing skill. Once you surpass this goal, you will be notified." The manual's advice: "Your first goal should be to type at about one keystroke per second. This means typing at a speed of about twelve WPM. Once you are able to type at this speed, increase your speed to fifteen WPM, or let Mavis challenge you."
- 1992: "The speed goal is used by Mavis to judge your progress. Mavis will periodically adjust this number based on your performance."
- 2005: the goal applies only once all keys are introduced (section 2).
- 2008: default goals of 30 or 40 WPM; 2015: the default is 40 WPM, "with typing goals as low as 10 words per minute"; 2020: "The default setting is 40 WPM, but you can set the slider for any value between 25 WPM and 120 WPM… Once you have obtained your goal, it is a good idea to adjust your WPM to a greater value".
- UltraKey: "Success-based learning begins with setting a personal challenge level for speed and accuracy that you want to achieve as you complete lessons."

**What is measured and shown.**

- 1987: "Speed, Accuracy, and Completion meters on-screen"; "Over 20 colorful graphs"; "Show Proficient Keys displays the keys that you type faster than your target speed"; a printable "Report Card".
- 1992: the graphs include Progress ("adjusted speed (including a penalty for errors) for up to 26 of you most recent sessions"), Adjusted Speed ("penalizes you for errors by subtracting one keystroke for each mistake"), per-key Speed ("while ignoring errors"), per-key Accuracy ("the percentage of the time you type it correctly"), Problem Keys, and the error graphs of section 3.
- 2008: per player, "Speed Goal", "WPM", "AWPM" ("typing speeds after they are adjusted for errors"), "Total Time Played", "% Accuracy" ("the percent of characters they have typed correctly during all of their quizzes and tests"), and the number of quizzes and speed tests. A "Progress Over Time" chart has one column per quiz or test (Goal, WPM and AWPM); 2005 caps it at 75 entries.
- Key proficiency (2008, 2020): keys coloured red ("having trouble with… Red keys can also be keys that your student has not used enough"), blue ("know fairly well but has not mastered") and green ("consistently presses these keys correctly during lessons, quizzes, and games"). Hovering over a key shows "why your student may be pressing these keys incorrectly".
- Curriculum map and certificates (2008, 2020): a certificate per finished level; the completion certificate when the player "has reached the Advanced level and achieved his or her typing goal". In 2020 it "certifies the number of hours you have trained with Mavis Beacon". In 2026 the certificate comes "after 8 hours of practice".

**Practice time.** 1987, 1995 and 2008 all say: "Studies have shown that typing is learned more efficiently if you practice for about one hour a day; not more, not less… You won't learn any faster if you spend eight hours in front of your keyboard". No study is named. The 1987 setup sets a default practice time of 30 minutes, after which "an alarm is posted that lets you know you have worked enough for the session". Platinum 20's page claims results "in 2 weeks guaranteed"; UltraKey's claims "Proven positive results in just eight hours of step-by-step instruction".

_(review)_ COMPUTE! 1992: "Keys typed after a mistyped letter are read as multiple errors until the correct letter is input." Macworld 2009: the floating WPM gauge "only judges by keystrokes, not actual words, so, it's possible to get well over 100 words per minute by simply typing gibberish". START 1989 asked for a 30-minute session and found "the program didn't warn me that the time was up".

### Inference

- **Mavis's first goal is the simulated beginner's speed.** One keystroke a second is 12 WPM, the speed of the simulated beginner in the map's Notes. Mavis treats it as a first goal, then 15 WPM. That is one more data point beside [Accuracy and speed benchmarks](beginner-typing-benchmarks.md) for fair WPM bars on early steps.
- **Mavis's reported accuracy counts only quizzes and tests.** Its "% Accuracy" and its progress chart leave practice lessons and games out, while its key colours draw on everything. Phase 3 could ask the same question: which Runs feed what the orchestrator reads, and which feed what the player is shown.
- **The two measuring faults the reviewers named are ones astro-type has already fixed.** Counting every key after a mistake as another error is what P10's First attempts avoid. A WPM gauge that rises with gibberish can't happen in astro-type, where a wrong key never moves the text on and WPM counts only the characters of the text typed (`grossWpm` in `src/game/rules.ts`).
- **"One hour a day" is unsupported in the sources,** and P7 asks for 15–20 minutes. Neither Mavis's figure nor its marketing claims ("2 weeks", "eight hours") are evidence for or against P7.

**Studies.** I searched ERIC (its API, for "Mavis Beacon" and "mavis"), PubMed and PubMed Central, and the web for studies that used it:

- **No peer-reviewed study of how well Mavis Beacon teaches turned up.** ERIC's two hits are a 1988 product review and a 2000 master's project ([ED441255](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED441255)) in which learners used Mavis Beacon before a writing study; the software wasn't what was tested. PubMed's hits use Mavis Beacon as a typing task in ergonomics studies, not as a teacher.
- **A 1995 Texas A&M master's thesis** compared typing classes on Mavis Beacon (1991 edition) and another program ([record](https://oaktrust.library.tamu.edu/handle/1969.1/ETD-TAMU-1995-THESIS-G66)). The repository blocked automated access, so I couldn't read its abstract or results, and search snippets of its abstract disagree about what it found. Not used.
- **A 2018 action-research paper** on student teachers in Ghana used Mavis Beacon with classroom demonstrations (_The International Journal of Science & Technoledge_, DOI 10.24940/theijst/2018/v6/i12/ST1812-024). I couldn't open it. Not used.
- **A 1995 master's thesis on ERIC** ([ED381132](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED381132.pdf)) that turned up in the search compares teacher-led and software teaching, but it mentions Mavis Beacon only as software some learners used at home. Not about Mavis.

### Inference

- **Reviewers liked the same things across 20 years:** analysis that finds weak keys quickly, mixing hard and easy lessons, and practice that keeps the eyes on the screen. They disliked gates that demand perfection and measures that count keys rather than correct characters.
- **Nothing in this research shows that Mavis Beacon's method teaches better or worse than a fixed course.** That matches the earlier finding in [What adaptive typing tutors read to choose practice](https://github.com/k-electron/astro-type/blob/research/adaptive-practice-data/docs/research/adaptive-practice-data.md) that no controlled study compares adaptive practice with a fixed curriculum. Mavis's commercial success is not evidence of how well it teaches.

## Questions for the decision tickets

These are inputs, not decisions.

1. **The path's shape:** should the letter steps follow Mavis Beacon's mirrored finger pairs (`ei`, `gh`, `ru`, `ty`, `qwop`, `vbmn`: ten lessons for all the letters), or bring in one hand's keys at a time? Mirrored pairs need fewer steps but may invite mirror Mix-ups; the simulated beginner can compare the two.
2. **The path's shape:** Mavis puts both Shift keys straight after the letters, then sentence punctuation (`?":!`), then digits in groups of two or three, then symbols. Does Phase 3 copy that order between Shift and digits?
3. **Progress:** Mavis's step is optional practice plus a quiz that can't be skipped. Is a Phase 3 step's gate one Stage (a quiz), or Stars on any of the step's Stages? And how high is the gate? The one reviewer who mentioned the quiz bar called 100% obsessive.
4. **Progress (owner's call):** Mavis asks for no speed until every key has been introduced. Should WPM bars on early steps be lower, or absent? This would change Star bars, so it is the owner's decision.
5. **The orchestrator's evidence:** keep Mavis's third key weight (favour keys rarely practised, so every key keeps coming up) beside the vision's Stage keys, weak keys and English frequency?
6. **The orchestrator's evidence:** choose a loop by kind of mistake first (a standout Mix-up pattern: wrong row, wrong column, mirror, Shift), then by finger, row or hand, then a general loop, as Mavis does?
7. **What the player sees:** Mavis's Chalkboard shows the problem, the remedy and a longer reason, with "another suggestion" and "repeat" beside it. Is that the model for the suggested next step?
8. **Frustration:** Mavis's three signs are the vision's three. Mavis's answers were a game, a lower goal, or "calling it a day". Which of these can astro-type's orchestrator offer, given that every Stage is already a game?
9. **The library and generator:** which of Mavis's formats become loop Stage kinds: Pattern (common letter sequences, the nearest to Letter-pair loops), Barrier (read ahead in chunks), Shift Key Drill (capitals), Guided Key Tour (finger to key)?
10. **The Logbook:** Mavis's accuracy report counts quizzes and tests only. Should every Run (main path and loop) count the same toward what the orchestrator reads and what the Debrief and Analytics show?
